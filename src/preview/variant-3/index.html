Abstract

The Public Celtic Encyclopedia is an accessioned, cited, and freely-licensed reference to the deities, festivals, sacred places, and textual sources of seven Celtic cultures. Praxis complements the encyclopedia with 113 ritual guides — attested, reconstructed, and clearly labelled. Corrections are welcome; provenance is required.

Part I. The Public Celtic Encyclopedia

Cultural areas

The encyclopedia is arranged first by culture, then by class of entry — deity, festival, hero, site, source. Each entry is accessioned; each cites its manuscripts and identifies its uncertainties. Below: the seven cultural areas presently maintained, with entry counts.

Irish · Deity · Entry no. 017

Brigit of the three flames

A triadic figure in the medieval glosses: poetry, healing, smithcraft. Attested in Cormac's Glossary (c. 900) and cross-identified with the saint of Kildare in the hagiographic tradition. The site cross-lists surviving well-blessings under Praxis.

Gaulish · Calendar · Entry no. 081

The Coligny calendar

A bronze lunisolar calendar excavated near Coligny, France, in 1897; the longest continuous document in Gaulish. Its five-year intercalary cycle underwrites much modern reconstructionist practice; the entry gives the tablet reading in full.

Read entry →

Welsh · Text · Entry no. 064

The Four Branches of the Mabinogi

The medieval prose cycles preserved in the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest. This entry treats Pwyll, Branwen, Manawydan, and Math as a single work, noting attributed dating and manuscript variants.

Read entry →

Scottish · Text · Entry no. 022

The Carmina Gadelica

Alexander Carmichael's late nineteenth-century collection of Hebridean prayers and charms. The entry marks the editorial choices, the survivals of pre-Christian address, and the tension between ethnography and reconstruction.

Part II. Praxis

Ritual guides

One hundred and thirteen guides for the wheel of the year, for household devotion, for hearth-keeping. Each is annotated: what is attested, what is reconstructed, and what has been chosen for our own time.

Fire festival · Guide P-042

A hearth-fire kindled before dawn on the first of May: the ordering of the need-fires, the driving of cattle, the household blessing.

Editors' preface · § 3

Why the archive is open.

A living tradition is a plural one. We publish the sources, the disagreements, and the working notes so any reader may weigh them for themselves.

There is no single Celtic orthodoxy — there never was. What we offer is a printed archive's honesty: a citation for every claim, an accession record for every source, and clear notation of what remains uncertain. Corrections are welcome; provenance is required.
